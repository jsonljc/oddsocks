# ODD SOCKS — baseline simulation findings

**Date:** 2026-07-26
**Instrument:** `npm run sweep` (`src/cli/sweep.ts`), heuristic bots on both sides, Hollow House
**Sample:** 12 configurations × 2,000 games = 24,000 games, seed base 0 (deterministic, reproducible)
**Wall-clock:** 5.1–5.2s for the full matrix (measured twice; a 50-games/cell timing probe first
extrapolated ~7s, so N was not reduced — 2,000/cell, as the brief specified, ran comfortably)

This reads the sweep table at the bottom. Read the five limits in the last section before acting on
anything above them — they change what several of these numbers are allowed to mean.

---

## The headline: there is really one win condition, and the dodge isn't what's costing the race

`caught` is 0 in every one of the 24,000 games. That is not a bug — it is §7's dodge working exactly
as written. A Call is public; the villain sees its own name on it and walks somewhere else. Rules §1
advertises two children's win routes, catch or survive; against a villain that never fails to notice,
there is effectively one. The question worth asking is not "how often do Calls catch" — it's
**whether forcing that dodge costs the villain enough tempo to lose the race anyway.**

The direct test the brief specifies is `villain` in `baseline` (2-hand Calls) versus `call-hands-3`
(3-hand Calls, much harder to assemble) — all four numbers below are columns in the shipped table,
not a side computation:

| | `villain` | `live` | `thefts` (of 5 needed) | `quota` (reached all 5) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `baseline` | 9.4% | 0.733 | 3.37 | 9.4% (188/2,000) |
| `call-hands-3` | 8.9% | 0.223 | 3.34 | 8.9% (179/2,000) |

Raising the hand requirement to 3 cuts live Calls by ~70% — the villain is dodging far less often —
and villain win rate does not move (0.5 points, well inside the ~0.6-point sampling noise at N=2,000
per cell). **This sweep finds no measurable cost from dodging.** (`quota` and `villain` read
identically in both rows here — expected, since neither config lets the villain self-snuff, the one
way a game can rack up theft-type events without them all counting toward the win condition; the two
columns diverge for exactly that reason under `hush-silent-freeSnuff`, see Q6.)

But read that alongside `thefts`: the villain is landing barely two-thirds of the five thefts it
needs, in *both* configurations, and reaches all five in under 1 game in 10 either way. The shortfall
is nearly identical whether Calls are common or rare. That is the honest reading: **something other
than Call-dodging is already capping the villain's success rate**, so this comparison cannot show a
dodge-cost even if the design intends one.

The likely culprit is bot quality, not the rules: `heuristicBot.dusk` is the same function for all
six seats, driven purely by "do I want an item" — it has no theft-target logic at all. Villain-specific
reasoning only exists in `midnight`, one phase too late to correct a dusk position that wasn't chosen
with a target bedroom in mind. When nothing is wanted, `dusk` scores every legal path equally and
picks uniformly at random. A villain that can't reliably be in position to strike is a villain whose
win rate is capped by its own approach, before the Call mechanic gets a chance to matter. **This
sweep cannot distinguish "dodging doesn't cost the villain" from "the villain never gets far enough
for a dodge to be the thing that stops it."** Don't read the flat 9.4%→8.9% as a design verdict on
the Call; read it as inconclusive, and re-run this specific comparison against a villain with real
approach planning before trusting either direction.

## Correction: "Odd Socks can always find a safe lie" is not something this sweep measured

An earlier draft of this document reported `neverForcedRate = 1.000` in all 12 cells as the answer to
"can Odd Socks always find a safe lie" and treated it as the sweep's cleanest empirical result. That
was wrong, and it's my mistake in how the instrument was built, not a bot-quality caveat like the
ones above.

`forcedNight` can never be non-null, for any config, any bot, any seed — not because the villain
plays well, but because `solve` was built so that the villain's *true* claim history is always
internally consistent with itself. `src/analysis/safeLies.test.ts` states this as the solver's own
correctness check, not as an interesting property of the game: *"The true claim history is always
consistent, so no game can be 'forced' unless a constraint is wrong"* (the test asserts
`forcedNight` is `null` across 100 seeds, unconditionally). So `neverForcedRate = 1.000` and
`medianForcedNight = —` in every cell of this matrix were never in doubt before a single game was
run. Twelve configurations could not have disagreed. Reporting that agreement as a finding invited
exactly the wrong reading — "we tried hard to break the villain's cover and couldn't" — when what
actually happened is "the instrument cannot express that outcome at all."

**The real question was always narrower: not whether a safe lie exists, but whether the *only*
surviving one is the incriminating truth, on a night where that actually costs the villain
something.** On a theft night the villain is standing in the robbed bedroom; a claim history that has
narrowed to exactly that one room is a confession in every way that matters, even though `solve` still
calls it "not forced." Three new columns measure this — `collapse` (share of games where the viable
set ever narrows to exactly one room), `medColl` (the median night that first happens), and `lateColl`
(share of games where that narrowing happens on night 2 or later, i.e. excludes night 1) — all computed
by scanning the same `hidingSpace` array the table already carried; no solver change was needed or made.

**`lateColl` is the number to lead with: 50.5% for baseline.** Roughly one game in two sees the
villain's cover narrow down to a literal confession on a night when a theft, a marking, or a Call was
actually live — a real exposure, not a hypothetical one. That is a substantial finding on its own, and
a far more honest one than the raw `collapse` figure below, which badly overstates how often the
villain is in genuine danger.

**`collapse` alone would have overstated this, and the gap shows exactly by how much.** `collapse`
runs 85–98% across the matrix (baseline: 92.0%) — but `medColl` is **1** in every single cell, and
pulling the full distribution behind that median for baseline (N=2,000, same seeds as the shipped
table) shows why: **1,702 of 2,000 games (85.1%) have their first collapse on night 1 itself**, before
a single light has been stolen; the remaining collapses trickle across nights 2–7 (12, 24, 25, 31, 28,
19 games respectively), and 159 games (8.0%, matching `1 − collapse` within display rounding) never
collapse at all. Night 1 has no theft, no marking, no Call — a collapse there is free. That is
essentially the whole gap between the two figures: of `collapse`'s 92.0 points, about 41.5 are night-1
noise, leaving the 50.5 that `lateColl` isolates as actually meaningful.

Night 1 dominates `collapse` because it is, mechanically, the single most exposed night in the game —
through the **midnight** phase specifically, not "either phase." Only midnight sightings ever become
public testimony: `runMorning` builds every `reported` event from `midnight.sightings`; its dusk-phase
parameter is literally named `_dusk` and never read. `isLit` returns `true` unconditionally for every
non-bedroom room, and on night 1 every *bedroom* is lit too (nobody has been robbed yet), so any two of
the six players who end **midnight** together, anywhere in the house, walk away having named each
other — one positioning decision, not two, but a far bigger exposure surface than any later night,
where enough bedrooms have gone dark that camouflage exists and the trail's narrower, one-witness
mechanic takes over instead.

**A collapse — early or late — is a close call passed, not a trap that stays shut.** `hidingSpace` is
not monotonic; a pin to one room doesn't stay a pin, and each later night reopens the viable set to
whatever a fresh 4-hop walk and that night's own (often much weaker) constraints allow. That's why
`hiding` (mean rooms viable, averaged over the whole game) sits at **5.35** for baseline even though
half of all games hit a *meaningful* collapse and 92% hit *some* collapse — most of those games
recovered real breathing room afterward. `lateColl` answers "how often is the villain's story pinned
down on a night that matters"; `hiding` answers "how much average cover they're playing with across the
whole game" — related, not identical.

The Hush's three modes illustrate the same split. `hush-oneNight` and `hush-none` push `lateColl` to
**~52%** (0.520/0.521) against `silent`'s (baseline's) **50.5%** — a modest, directionally consistent
bump (roughly 1.3 standard errors at this sample size, not a large effect) in the expected direction:
less silencing means more testimony keeps arriving in later nights to re-narrow the villain's claim
history. All three sit close together on raw `collapse` too (~92%), because that figure is still
mostly set by night 1, before any Hush mode has had a chance to act. `hiding` moves far more (4.40 vs.
**5.35**) than either collapse figure does — `silent`'s permanent silencing is doing real, measurable
work on average cover over the whole game, more than on the specific odds of a late, meaningful pin.

## The six open questions

### 1. Can Odd Socks always find a safe lie?

Trivially, yes — but that answer is guaranteed by how the solver is built, not measured by this
sweep. `neverForcedRate = 1.000` / `medianForcedNight = —` in all 12 cells is a restatement of
`solve`'s own truth-preservation invariant (the villain's *true* history is always self-consistent,
so `forcedNight` can never be non-null — see `safeLies.test.ts`, and the correction section above),
not evidence about this design specifically. It would read exactly the same in any config, on any
map, with any bots.

The question worth asking instead — does the villain's cover ever narrow down to nothing but the
truth, on a night where that costs them something — has a real, config-sensitive answer: **`lateColl`
= 50.5%** for baseline. Roughly half of all games see a genuine, consequential collapse to the true
room on night 2 or later. The raw `collapse` figure (85–98% across the matrix, 92.0% at baseline) looks
far more dramatic but is mostly free exposure — 85.1% of baseline's collapses land on night 1, before
any theft, marking, or Call is even live. It doesn't stick either way — mean hiding space recovers to
**5.35** rooms afterward. See the correction section above for the full numbers and the mechanism
behind them; this entry exists so a reader jumping straight to "Q1" doesn't land on the misleading
version.

### 2. Does the trail identify anyone?

Yes, well above chance. Baseline trail accuracy is **53.3%**, against 16.7% (1-in-6) for guessing —
about 3.2× better than random, and it is the villain's *only* exposure on a theft night (sightings
resolve after the theft, in a room that has just gone dark — §3.4). The radius sweep shows the curve
is flat, then falls off a cliff: **54.0%** at `trail-radius-0` (name only someone who ended midnight
in the robbed room itself) is statistically indistinguishable from baseline's 53.3% at radius 1 —
adding the robbed room's immediate neighbours to the pool costs almost nothing — but accuracy drops
sharply to **40.9%** at `trail-radius-2` (name anyone within two rooms). The default radius of 1 is
sitting on the flat part of that curve, not yet paying for the extra reach; radius 2 clearly would be
paying for it. (For contrast, `core-only` — items, oddities and marking all off, radius still at the
default 1 — reads **50.3%**, a few points below baseline; that gap belongs to the layers comparison
below, not to the radius question.)

`lateColl` makes the same radius comparison far more dramatic than trail accuracy alone suggests:
**90.0%** at `trail-radius-0`, **50.5%** at baseline (radius 1), and **14.4%** at `trail-radius-2` — a
75.6-point swing end to end, versus `collapse`'s comparatively muted 98.1% → 92.0% → 87.5% (night 1's
free exposure floors all three close together regardless of radius). The end-to-end trail-accuracy gap
across the same two extremes is only ~13 points (54.0% at radius 0 to 40.9% at radius 2) — a
~13-point accuracy swing corresponds to a ~76-point swing in how often the villain is genuinely,
consequentially pinned down. The trail's precision compounds night over night in exactly the place
`collapse` alone couldn't show it.

### 3. Can a Call ever assemble two hands?

Yes, but it's the exception, not the rule. Baseline: **4.66 Calls posted per game**, of which
**0.73 go live** — roughly one live Call in six posted. `meanItemHolders` (`holders`) is **0.679**,
under one item-holder in the whole six-person roster on an average night — that thin pool is why
most Calls fizzle for lack of a second hand, not because nobody bothers to post one. `items-on-map-2`
confirms the pool is the constraint: fewer loose items → `holders` drops to 0.596 → `live` drops to
0.674; `items-on-map-4` barely helps further (`holders` 0.709, `live` 0.731), suggesting the default
of 3 is already close to where more items on the map stop buying anything.

### 4. Does dodging cost the villain the game?

Not measurably — see the headline above. `villain`: 9.4% (`baseline`) vs. 8.9% (`call-hands-3`),
a gap smaller than sampling noise, despite live Calls dropping ~70%. Treat this as inconclusive
rather than a clean "no" — the villain's win rate looks capped by something upstream of the Call
mechanic (see the bot-quality note above), so this A/B cannot yet isolate the dodge's true cost.

### 5. Does marking dominate?

No. `baseline` (marking on) vs. `marking-off`: villain win rate 9.4% vs. 9.6%, survived 90.6% vs.
90.4%, hiding space 5.352 vs. 5.363, trail accuracy 0.533 vs. 0.533 — all flat. The only effect
marking has anywhere in this matrix is a small drag on the live-Call rate (0.733 vs. 0.761, about
4% relative), consistent with marked item-holders being unable to join a Call, exactly as designed.
Isolating it more precisely — `core+items` (no oddities, no marking) vs. `marking-off` (oddities on,
marking still off) vs. `baseline` (both on) — the oddities layer alone accounts for essentially all
of the hiding-space drop (6.399 → 5.363), and marking on top of that moves nothing except the same
small live-Call dip (5.363 hiding, 0.761 live → 5.352 hiding, 0.733 live). Marking is not dominant;
if anything it is the layer with the least evidence behind it in this matrix (see below).

### 6. What are the side win rates, and by which route?

Across every cell: `caught` is 0%, `survived` is ~87–93%, and `villain` (lightsOut) is the remainder,
~7–13%. Concretely at baseline: villain 9.4%, caught 0%, survived 90.6%. The overwhelming majority of
children's wins are "the villain ran out of nights," not "the villain was trapped." The free-snuff
Hush exploit is the one cell that shifts this meaningfully: `hush-silent-freeSnuff` (self-snuffing
buys `activeNights + 1` instead of costing a night) pushes villain win rate to **12.9%**, up 3.5
points from baseline — a real, sizeable move at this sample size, and the clearest confirmation in
the whole matrix that the free-tempo exploit §3.1 flagged actually does what the concern said it
would.

It's also the one cell where `quota` (20.7%) and `villain` (12.9%) visibly pull apart — everywhere
else in the table they read identically. The self-snuff itself counts as one of the `thefts` a game
needs to reach its quota, but does not by itself darken one of the five bedrooms the win condition
counts (the villain's own bedroom is deliberately excluded from `darkBedroomCount`, so cover doesn't
double as progress). Reading the two side by side: `quota` rises 11.3 points over baseline
(9.4% → 20.7%) but `villain` rises only 3.5 points (9.4% → 12.9%) — most of the apparent quota gain
(7.8 of the 11.3 points) doesn't cash out as a win, consistent with a good share of those "extra"
theft-type events being the self-snuff itself rather than a fifth productive theft.

## Which layers earned their place

Comparing the three build rungs — `core-only` → `core+items` → `baseline` (+oddities, +marking):

| | villain | survived | thefts | collapse | lateColl | hiding | trailAcc | posted | live | holders |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `core-only` | 7.2% | 92.8% | 3.280 | 85.1% | 16.7% | 6.432 | 50.3% | 3.047 | 0.414 | 0 |
| `core+items` | 9.3% | 90.7% | 3.374 | 85.2% | 17.6% | 6.399 | 53.6% | 4.666 | 0.784 | 0.689 |
| `baseline` | 9.4% | 90.6% | 3.372 | 92.0% | 50.5% | 5.352 | 53.3% | 4.663 | 0.733 | 0.679 |

**Items clearly earn their place.** Adding them very nearly doubles the live-Call rate (0.414 →
0.784) and pushes posted Calls up 53% (3.05 → 4.67) — the heuristic children only propose a Call
when they personally hold an item to back it (`suspectCall`/`selfNominate` both gate on
`held.length > 0`), so without items the accusation mechanic is close to inert, not merely
under-armed. It also moves villain tempo a real amount (`thefts` 3.280 → 3.374, `villain` 7.2% →
9.3%) while barely moving `collapse` (85.1% → 85.2%) or `lateColl` (16.7% → 17.6%) — items change
whether the villain gets away with a theft, not how exposed their story is.

**Oddities earn a much stronger place than the raw `collapse` figure alone showed.** Isolated cleanly
(`core+items` → `marking-off`, which differs only in the oddities flag — `marking-off`'s `lateColl` is
50.5%, identical to baseline's, since marking on top changes nothing further), `lateColl` very nearly
**triples**: **17.6% → 50.5%**, a swing `collapse` almost entirely hid (85.2% → 92.2%, because night
1's free exposure already floors both numbers near their ceiling). Oddities also account for
essentially the entire hiding-space drop from rung 2 to baseline (`core+items` 6.399 → `marking-off`
5.363, ~16%; marking on top moves it only to baseline's 5.352) — real, consequential constraining
power on the villain's cover — but move `thefts`, `villain`, `live`, and `trailAcc` by nothing worth
calling a finding. Items move tempo and outcomes; oddities move meaningful exposure. Between them, the
two rungs cover different parts of "does the evidence engine work" — neither one alone, and neither
the raw `collapse` figure alone, would have shown the whole picture.

**Marking is the weakest layer measured here.** Isolated (`marking-off` → `baseline`), its only
visible effect anywhere in this matrix is the ~4%-relative live-Call dip noted under Q5 — `thefts`,
`collapse`, `lateColl`, `villain`, `survival`, `hiding`, and `trailAcc` are all flat across that
comparison too (`lateColl` reads 50.5% on both sides, to three decimal places). It does not move any
metric this sweep tracks beyond that one small Call-joining dip. It is the strongest single candidate
for a complexity cut before a human plays: three configurations bracket it (`core-only` has it off
structurally, `marking-off` isolates it explicitly, `baseline` turns it on) and none show it doing
more than trim an already-thin Call-joining pool a little further.

One footnote on reading the `core-only` row: `holders` reads exactly **0** there — not "close to
zero," genuinely zero — because `layers.items = false` disables both map-spawning and pickup
(`spawnItems`/`resolvePickups` both short-circuit), and `meanItemHolders` only counts `itemTaken`
events. But `live` is still 0.414, not 0: the Grip (`theft.ts`) hands a robbed player an item
straight from the reserve when the villain wasn't already carrying one, and that path is **not**
gated by the items layer — it's core-rung behaviour per the build order in the spec (§8 lists Grip
under rung 1). So a trickle of item-holders exists even with the items layer off; `holders` just
can't see it, because it only counts one of the two ways to end up holding something. Worth knowing
before reading `core-only`'s `holders` column as "nobody could join a Call that night" — two people
evidently did, in the sample checked by hand.

## What this harness cannot see

Five limits that change what the numbers above are allowed to mean. These are not hedges to skim —
a reader who acts on this document without them will draw conclusions the data doesn't support.

1. **`encntr` (encounter rate) is the least trustworthy column in the table and should not be
   argued from.** It sits at 0.061–0.074 everywhere, barely moving across 12 very different
   configurations — which is itself the tell. Heuristic bots do not cluster, follow, or camp a
   room the way six people around a real house would; this number reflects bot movement habits far
   more than it reflects the design. Reported because the brief asks for it, not because it settles
   anything.

2. **Every result above is an upper bound on available signal, not an estimate of it.** R15: every
   child who can report, does — truthfully, immediately, with no utterance budget. The real game
   gives each player four utterances a morning and the choice of what to spend them on. A real table
   will produce less signal than this harness ever can, not more. Read every number here as "at
   best," never as "expect this."

3. **The morning is entirely unmodelled.** No board, no six-symbol language, no arguing about whose
   claim to trust, no persuasion. The harness validates the night's information economy — whether a
   safe lie exists, whether a Call can physically assemble — and is silent on whether the social game
   built on top of that information actually plays well. A clean baseline here is not evidence the
   full game works; it's evidence the plumbing underneath it does.

4. **Bot-independence and meaningfulness are two different properties, and this document previously
   conflated them for `forcedNight`.** `forcedNight`, `hidingSpace`, `collapseRate`,
   `medianCollapseNight`, and `lateCollapseRate` all come from `solve`, which computes everything from
   ground truth and never reads what the villain bot actually claimed — so none of the five are
   contaminated by the villain's lying quality (roughly half its attempted lies land outside the
   solver's own safe set, per Task 18). But bot-independence only guarantees a number is
   *reproducible*, not that it's *informative*. `forcedNight`/`neverForcedRate` are bot-independent
   **and structurally constant** — `solve`'s truth-preservation invariant fixes them at "never"/`1.000`
   before any config is chosen (see the correction section above and Q1).
   `hidingSpace`/`collapseRate`/`medianCollapseNight`/`lateCollapseRate` are bot-independent **and
   genuinely config-sensitive** — they vary in real, explainable ways across this matrix (oddities lift
   `collapse` a little and `lateColl` a lot; Hush mode moves `hiding` more than either collapse figure;
   trail radius moves `lateColl` far more sharply than it moves `collapse`). Trust all five not to be
   artifacts of bad villain lying; trust only the latter four to tell you anything, and `lateColl`
   specifically over `collapse` when the two disagree about how alarming a number looks. Every win-rate
   number in this document (`villain`, `caught`, `survived`, `thefts`, `quota`, and by extension the
   whole "does dodging cost the villain" question) comes from bots actually playing the game out, and
   is contaminated by both sides' bot quality — most concretely the villain's dusk-phase approach
   weakness documented in the headline section. Treat the safe-lie/collapse numbers as findings about
   the design. Treat the win-rate numbers as findings about the design *as played by these bots*,
   pending a stronger villain.

5. **`caught = 0` is a property of rational dodging, not a measurement failure.** It's covered in
   the headline above, but it earns its own line here too: this was independently confirmed by code
   review in Task 18, not left unverified. Nothing in a future pass should try to force it non-zero —
   a non-zero `caught` rate against a villain that always notices its own name would itself be the
   bug.

## What comes after this document

Not this task's call to make, but worth stating plainly since two specific outcomes were flagged in
advance as the load-bearing branch points:

- **"Never forced into a contradiction" cannot be the branch point — it was never going to be
  otherwise, in this matrix or any other.** The real open question is whether `lateColl` (50.5% at
  baseline — a genuine, consequential exposure on roughly half of all games) is itself a bot-quality
  artifact, at least in part. This sweep's villain wanders into shared midnight rooms largely without
  any theft-approach planning (see the headline's dusk-phase note); a villain with real approach

  villain bot before concluding how much of this design's real, late-game exposure is structural
  versus an artifact of how weakly this one plays it.
- **Calls do assemble** (Q3 came back "yes," not "no") — the item-holder pool is thin but not empty,
  and the mechanism isn't broken, just infrequent. §12 Q2 does not need the emergency fix ("cheaper
  join cost or more items") the spec flagged as one of the two likely outcomes; `items-on-map-4`

- **Marking is the cleanest complexity-cut candidate** this sweep found — see "Which layers earned

- **The dodge-cost question (Q4) is unresolved, not answered "no."** Before concluding the Call's
